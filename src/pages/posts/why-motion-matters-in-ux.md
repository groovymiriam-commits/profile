There are so many AI tools now that keeping track of them has practically become a hobby.

Every week, there's a new model, a new feature, or another company promising that its AI will change the way you work forever.

But here's something worth remembering: not every AI is good at everything.

Some are great at generating images. Others are useful for coding, writing, research, or creating videos. And some are particularly interesting because you can run their models locally or integrate them into your own applications.

There are also some excellent AI tools coming from China, including DeepSeek, Qwen, Kimi, and Kling. They're worth knowing about, especially if you work in development or creative production.

So instead of trying every new AI tool that appears on your feed, let's look at what the major options actually do and when you might want to use them.

## Gemini: Great for Image Generation and Visual Exploration

Google's Gemini is worth exploring if you work with images, design, or creative projects.

Depending on the model and features available, Gemini can generate and edit images, analyze visual references, work with documents, and help you explore creative concepts.

Imagine you're designing a website and need a hero illustration that fits your visual identity.

Instead of searching through hundreds of stock images, you can describe the composition, colors, lighting, and style you're looking for. You can also provide an existing image and ask for specific changes.

This makes Gemini useful for exploring different art directions, creating visual references, and experimenting with ideas before committing to a final design.

### What Gemini is good for

- Generating and editing images.
- Exploring visual concepts and art directions.
- Analyzing images and screenshots.
- Working with text, images, and other supported input formats.
- Brainstorming ideas for websites and creative projects.

**My take:** Gemini is worth trying when your work involves visual exploration. It's useful for moving from a rough idea to a visual concept without creating every element yourself.

However, image generation isn't exclusive to Gemini. ChatGPT also offers image generation and editing, and other specialized tools can be better suited to particular visual styles or workflows.

The best choice depends on what you're trying to create.

## ChatGPT: Your General-Purpose Creative Assistant

ChatGPT is a good starting point when you need an AI that can help with several different parts of a project.

You can use it to brainstorm ideas, write content, explain technical concepts, analyze files, generate images, troubleshoot code, and work through complicated problems.

One of its biggest advantages is that you can move between these tasks in one conversation.

For example, you could start by discussing a website concept, develop the content structure, refine the copy, generate visual references, and then work on the implementation.

You don't necessarily need a separate tool for every stage.

### What ChatGPT is good for

- Brainstorming and developing ideas.
- Writing and editing content.
- Explaining technical concepts.
- Generating and editing images.
- Analyzing documents and data.
- Coding and debugging.
- Planning and structuring projects.

**My take:** ChatGPT is a strong general-purpose option, especially when you want to develop an idea through conversation and gradually turn it into something practical.

It's also useful when you don't know exactly what you need yet. You can start with a rough idea, ask questions, and refine the direction as you go.

Just remember that a confident answer isn't necessarily a correct one. Important facts and technical recommendations still need checking.

## Claude: For Writing, Code, and Complex Projects

Claude, developed by Anthropic, is another tool worth knowing about, particularly if your work involves writing, analyzing documents, or developing software.

It's useful for reviewing code, explaining existing projects, improving long pieces of writing, and thinking through implementation decisions.

For developers, one particularly useful workflow is giving Claude relevant project context and asking it to improve an existing implementation rather than generating an entirely new one.

For example, you could ask it to review a React component, identify unnecessary complexity, and suggest a cleaner implementation.

The same approach works for writing.

You can provide an existing article and ask for improvements to its structure, clarity, or tone without necessarily rewriting everything.

### What Claude is good for

- Writing and editing longer pieces of content.
- Reviewing and explaining code.
- Refactoring existing implementations.
- Working through complex technical problems.
- Analyzing documents and large amounts of text.
- Supporting coding workflows that involve multiple steps.

**My take:** Claude is worth trying when you're working with substantial pieces of writing or code and want help understanding and improving them.

However, ChatGPT can handle these tasks too. Neither tool automatically produces better results for every project, so compare them using your actual work rather than relying on online rankings.

## DeepSeek: A Strong Option for Coding and Reasoning

DeepSeek is a Chinese AI company that has attracted attention for its language models, reasoning capabilities, and coding performance.

It's particularly interesting if you work with code, technical problems, or tasks that require structured reasoning.

DeepSeek offers models that can be used through its services, while some model releases also make their weights available for developers to run or adapt under the applicable license.

That creates different possibilities depending on how you want to work.

You can use an online assistant for everyday questions, integrate a supported model into an application, or explore running a suitable model in your own environment.

### What DeepSeek is good for

- Writing and explaining code.
- Working through mathematical and technical problems.
- Analyzing complex questions.
- Exploring alternative approaches to software development.
- Experimenting with AI models through APIs or local deployments where supported.

**My take:** DeepSeek is worth testing if you want another option for coding and technical problem-solving.

It's also an interesting example of how much competition there is in AI development. You don't have to limit yourself to American AI companies to find capable models.

Keep in mind that performance varies between model versions, and running a model locally requires suitable hardware. If you're working with confidential client information, also check the privacy and data policies of whichever service you use.

## Qwen: An Interesting Choice for Developers

Qwen is a family of AI models developed by Alibaba.

Like other major model families, it includes different models designed for different tasks. Depending on the version, Qwen can support text generation, coding, reasoning, image understanding, and other multimodal workflows.

What makes Qwen particularly interesting for developers is the availability of models that can be integrated into applications or deployed in different environments.

Some Qwen models have open weights, which gives developers more flexibility to experiment with self-hosting and customization, subject to the relevant license and hardware requirements.

### What Qwen is good for

- Coding and technical assistance.
- Text generation and analysis.
- Understanding supported image inputs.
- Building AI-powered applications.
- Experimenting with models that can run in different environments.

**My take:** Qwen is worth exploring if you're interested in development or want to experiment with AI beyond the usual chat interfaces.

It's also a useful option to investigate if you're interested in local AI, where a suitable model runs on your own hardware instead of sending every request to a cloud service.

Just remember that Qwen is a model family, not a single model with identical capabilities across every version.

## Kimi: For Research and Long Documents

Kimi is an AI assistant developed by the Chinese company Moonshot AI.

It's particularly interesting for people who work with large amounts of information or need help completing tasks that involve several steps.

Depending on the model and features available, Kimi supports capabilities such as web search, long-context conversations, multimodal understanding, and agent-based workflows.

Imagine you're researching an unfamiliar technology and need to understand its documentation, compare different approaches, and organize your findings.

An AI assistant with strong long-context capabilities can help you work through that material without treating every document as an entirely separate conversation.

### What Kimi is good for

- Researching topics using web search.
- Working with long documents and conversations.
- Analyzing information and developing ideas.
- Coding and technical tasks.
- Exploring workflows that involve multiple steps.

**My take:** Kimi is worth trying if you regularly work with lengthy documents or research-heavy projects.

It's another reminder that useful AI assistants aren't limited to the companies most familiar to Western users.

As with any AI research tool, verify important claims against the original sources. A long answer with several citations can still contain mistakes.

## Perplexity: When You Need Research, Not Guesswork

Sometimes you don't need an AI to invent something or write something beautifully.

You need it to find information.

That's where Perplexity can be useful.

It's an AI-powered search and research tool designed to answer questions using web sources. It provides citations that help you investigate where the information came from.

Imagine you're researching a JavaScript library, comparing hosting providers, or trying to understand the latest changes to a web development tool.

Instead of relying only on a chatbot's existing knowledge, you can use a search-focused tool to find relevant sources and follow the references.

### What Perplexity is good for

- Researching current topics.
- Finding relevant websites and documentation.
- Comparing products and services.
- Exploring unfamiliar technologies.
- Finding sources to support an article.
- Getting an overview of a topic before investigating it further.

**My take:** Perplexity is useful when the main job is finding and checking information.

But citations aren't a guarantee of accuracy. Sources can be outdated, irrelevant, or misinterpreted, so open the important links and verify the claims yourself.

You may not need a separate research tool for every question if your existing AI assistant already has web search.

## Midjourney: For Artistic Image Generation

If your main goal is creating visually distinctive images, Midjourney is worth exploring.

It's particularly associated with artistic image generation and offers ways to experiment with aesthetics, composition, lighting, and visual styles.

For designers, this can be useful during the early stages of a project.

You might use it to explore an art direction for a campaign, develop moodboard imagery, experiment with a character concept, or create visual references for a website.

Rather than committing immediately to one idea, you can explore several directions and see which one communicates the right feeling.

### What Midjourney is good for

- Artistic image generation.
- Exploring visual styles and aesthetics.
- Creating moodboard imagery.
- Developing visual concepts for campaigns.
- Experimenting with lighting and composition.
- Producing creative references for design projects.

**My take:** Midjourney is worth considering when the visual style itself is a major part of the project.

However, if you mainly need to edit an existing photograph, work with specific visual references, or move between image creation and general conversation, another tool might fit your workflow better.

The best option depends on what you need to produce, not just which tool creates the prettiest image in a comparison video.

## Kling AI: For AI-Generated Video

If you work with video, animation, or social media content, Kling AI is another tool worth exploring.

Developed by the Chinese technology company Kuaishou, Kling offers tools for generating video from text prompts and visual references.

Depending on the model and features available, you can create cinematic scenes, animate still images, experiment with camera movements, and develop visual concepts for video projects.

Imagine you have a still image of a character and want to turn it into a short animated scene.

Instead of building the entire sequence manually, you can use an image-to-video workflow to generate movement from the original image.

### What Kling AI is good for

- Generating videos from text prompts.
- Animating existing images.
- Exploring cinematic shots and camera movements.
- Creating visual concepts for social media.
- Experimenting with AI-generated video sequences.

**My take:** Kling is worth exploring if you're a video editor or content creator who wants to experiment with AI-generated footage.

It's particularly interesting for developing ideas and producing visual material that would otherwise require more time or resources.

However, generated footage can contain inconsistencies, and you may need several attempts to get the movement, composition, and continuity you want.

Treat it as another creative tool, not a replacement for editing and visual judgment.

## GitHub Copilot: AI Where You Actually Write Code

If you spend a lot of time in VS Code, GitHub Copilot is worth considering.

Unlike a general chatbot that you visit to ask questions, Copilot integrates AI assistance into your development workflow.

Depending on the features available, it can suggest code as you type, explain existing code, help debug problems, and assist with larger development tasks.

For example, imagine you're building a React component and need to add a responsive navigation menu.

You can use an AI assistant in your editor to help implement the component, suggest CSS, or explain an unfamiliar part of the codebase without constantly switching between your editor and a separate chat window.

Copilot also supports multiple AI models, depending on the available product features and plan.

### What GitHub Copilot is good for

- Code completion while you work.
- Writing and improving functions.
- Explaining unfamiliar code.
- Helping debug errors.
- Working with existing repositories.
- Supporting development directly inside your editor.

**My take:** Copilot is particularly useful when you want AI assistance inside your existing coding workflow.

But don't confuse generating code with understanding it. AI can introduce bugs, make incorrect assumptions about your project, and produce code that works in isolation but doesn't fit the rest of your application.

You still need to review what it changes and test the result.

Use this table as a starting point, not a universal law of artificial intelligence.

## Do You Actually Need All These AI Tools?

Probably not.

It's easy to get caught up in trying every new tool, especially when each one promises to make you faster or more productive.

But using six different AI tools doesn't automatically make your workflow better.

In fact, constantly switching between them can create more work than it saves.

A more practical approach is to start with one general-purpose assistant and add specialized tools when you encounter a specific limitation.

For example, a freelance web developer might use:

- **ChatGPT** for brainstorming, writing, planning, and general problem-solving.
- **Gemini** for image generation and visual exploration.
- **Claude** for reviewing complex code or working through longer writing tasks.
- **GitHub Copilot** for assistance directly inside the code editor.
- **Perplexity** for research that requires current web sources.

A video editor might use ChatGPT or Claude for scripting, Gemini or Midjourney for visual concepts, and Kling for generating video footage.

A developer interested in local AI might experiment with DeepSeek or Qwen, depending on the model's requirements and license.

You don't need to use every tool every day. You might use only one or two for weeks, then bring in another when a project calls for it.

The point is to build a workflow around your actual needs rather than collecting AI subscriptions like they're Pokémon.

## What About Running AI Locally?

There's another distinction worth understanding: using an AI service online isn't the same as running an AI model on your own computer.

Tools such as Ollama let you run supported language models locally, provided your hardware can handle them.

This is where models with available weights, including certain DeepSeek and Qwen models, become particularly interesting.

Instead of sending every prompt to a cloud-based service, you can run a compatible model on your own machine.

### Why run AI locally?

- You can experiment without relying entirely on a hosted chatbot.
- You have more control over your environment and configuration.
- Local processing can reduce the need to send prompts to an external service.
- You can integrate models into your own development workflows.
- You can experiment with AI without necessarily paying for every API request.

There are trade-offs, of course.

Local models can require substantial memory and processing power. Smaller models may also perform less effectively on certain tasks than larger hosted models.

And running a model locally doesn't automatically solve every privacy issue. Your surrounding software, extensions, integrations, and configuration still matter.

**My take:** Local AI is particularly interesting if you enjoy experimenting with technology or want more control over how you use models.

You don't need to replace every cloud service with a local model. Start with a specific task and see whether running it locally makes sense.

## One More Thing: The Prompt Still Matters

Choosing the right AI is only part of the equation.

The way you describe the task can make a significant difference to the result.

For example, asking an AI to create a website is very different from explaining the website's purpose, audience, visual direction, technical requirements, and constraints.

The same applies to coding.

"Fix this component" gives the AI very little context.

"Review this React component, identify why the mobile navigation breaks below 768px, and suggest the smallest change that fixes the issue without modifying the desktop layout" gives it a much clearer task.

You can also ask an AI to explain its assumptions, identify potential problems, or compare different approaches before making changes.

And if the first result isn't right, give specific feedback instead of starting over immediately.

The goal isn't to write the longest possible prompt. It's to provide the information that actually matters.

A good model with clear instructions will often be more useful than a more powerful model working from a vague request.

## Final Thoughts

AI tools are becoming increasingly capable, but that doesn't mean you need to use the most powerful model for every task.

Sometimes you need an image generator. Sometimes you need a coding assistant. Sometimes you need a research tool that helps you find reliable sources.

And sometimes you need to run a model locally because you want more control over your workflow.

The interesting part is that you no longer have to rely on a handful of companies. Tools developed in the United States, China, and elsewhere are competing across writing, coding, image generation, research, and video creation.

That competition gives you more options, but it also makes choosing the right tool more confusing.

The useful skill isn't memorizing every AI tool on the market. It's understanding what each one can do, recognizing its limitations, and knowing when to use it.

Start with the task. Choose the tool that fits. Check the result.

And remember that AI is supposed to make your work easier, not give you another full-time job managing AI tools.
