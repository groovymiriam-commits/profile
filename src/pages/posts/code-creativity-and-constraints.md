Creative work on the web always has constraints: a real deadline, a range of devices, an existing content system, or a browser that does not support every new idea. These limits can feel like friction, but they can also make the important decisions clearer.

## Begin with the outcome

When a requirement seems restrictive, step back and ask what it is protecting. A performance budget may be there to keep a page usable on a slower connection. A content limit may reflect how people will scan the page. Understanding the purpose of a constraint makes it easier to find a solution that respects it.

## Explore options at the right scale

Not every question needs a full prototype. A quick sketch can compare layouts; a small code experiment can reveal whether an interaction is feasible. Choose the cheapest way to learn something useful before committing to a detailed implementation.

This also helps separate the essential idea from its first expression. If an effect is difficult to support accessibly or responsively, the underlying purpose may have a simpler, stronger form.

## Let the medium contribute

The browser has its own strengths: responsive layout, semantic structure, real text, and interaction that can adapt to a person's device. Working with those strengths often produces a more resilient result than trying to make every screen behave like a static mockup.

## Treat trade-offs as design decisions

There is rarely one perfect answer. A project may choose a simpler animation to improve performance, or a flexible layout over a pixel-perfect arrangement. Making those trade-offs explicit helps the team keep the experience coherent—and helps the final work feel deliberate rather than compromised.
