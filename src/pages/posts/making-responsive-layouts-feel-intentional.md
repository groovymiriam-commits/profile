Let's be honest. AI can create some pretty impressive designs.

You can ask it to design a website, create a logo, generate an illustration, or build an entire visual identity in seconds. Things that used to take hours can now happen in a few minutes.

So why does so much AI-generated design look exactly the same?

The problem isn't necessarily the technology. It's how we use it.

AI can generate almost anything you want, but if you give it a vague prompt, you'll probably get a vague result. And when everyone asks for something modern, clean, minimal, and professional, it's hardly surprising that the results start looking identical.

The secret isn't avoiding AI. It's learning how to direct it.

## The Problem With Generic Prompts

Imagine asking an AI tool to design a homepage for your portfolio.

Your prompt is:

"Create a modern, clean, professional portfolio website with a nice color palette and interesting animations."

Sounds reasonable, right?

The result will probably look reasonable, too. Maybe a large heading, a gradient background, some rounded cards, a few floating shapes, and a button that says "Let's Work Together."

There's nothing necessarily wrong with that design. The problem is that it could belong to almost anyone.

You haven't told the AI anything about your personality, your audience, your visual preferences, or what makes your work different.

You've basically asked it to design something that looks good without explaining what "good" means to you.

And AI is very good at giving you the most obvious interpretation of what you ask for.

If you want something original, you need to give it more to work with.

## You Can Create Almost Anything With AI

Here's the thing: AI doesn't have to produce generic designs.

You can use it to explore unusual visual styles, experiment with typography, create illustrations, test color combinations, and develop layouts you might not have considered yourself.

You can ask for something playful, editorial, chaotic, nostalgic, futuristic, or deliberately imperfect.

The possibilities are enormous.

But there's a difference between asking AI to make something beautiful and explaining exactly what kind of beauty you're looking for.

For example, compare these two prompts.

**Prompt 1:**

"Design a creative portfolio website with a playful style, colorful illustrations, and modern typography."

**Prompt 2:**

"Design a portfolio homepage for a freelance frontend developer and video editor. The visual direction should feel like a creative studio's sketchbook mixed with an editorial magazine. Use a warm off-white background, dark forest-green typography, and muted terracotta accents. Combine bold, expressive headlines with handwritten annotations, subtle paper textures, and small hand-drawn illustrations. Use an asymmetrical layout with overlapping elements and slightly rotated project cards. The design should feel personal, curious, and a little unconventional, not like a corporate agency template. Avoid generic gradients, excessive rounded cards, glassmorphism, and predictable centered layouts. Keep the content readable, the navigation simple, and the mobile layout practical."

See the difference?

The first prompt describes a general aesthetic. The second gives the AI a direction.

It defines the audience, color palette, typography, layout, visual details, and even the things to avoid.

You're no longer asking for a nice website. You're describing a specific design concept.

And that's where things get interesting.

## How to Write Better Design Prompts

You don't need to become a prompt engineer or memorize some secret formula. You just need to learn how to communicate visual ideas more clearly.

Here are five things worth including in your next design prompt.

### 1. Describe the personality, not just the style

Words like "modern," "beautiful," and "professional" aren't particularly useful on their own.

They describe qualities that almost every design brief claims to want.

Instead, think about the feeling you want the design to create.

Should it feel playful and slightly chaotic? Calm and thoughtful? Bold and experimental? Nostalgic, like an old printed magazine?

Try describing the personality as if you were describing a person.

For example:

"Create a visual identity that feels curious, witty, and slightly rebellious. It should look like it belongs to someone who experiments with ideas rather than following every design trend."

That's much more useful than simply asking for a modern aesthetic.

### 2. Give it a specific visual direction

If you have a particular design style in mind, explain what makes that style work.

Instead of saying:

"Make it look editorial."

Try:

"Use the visual language of independent print magazines: oversized headlines, contrasting serif and sans-serif typography, asymmetrical layouts, generous whitespace, and small handwritten notes that add a personal touch."

You're breaking an abstract concept into actual visual decisions.

You can do the same with almost any aesthetic.

Want something retro? Describe the era, typography, colors, textures, and graphic elements you associate with it.

Want something minimalist? Explain whether you mean a quiet, monochromatic layout or a bold design with very few elements.

The more specific you are, the less the AI has to guess.

### 3. Tell it what NOT to do

This is one of the most useful parts of writing a design prompt.

AI tools often fall back on familiar design patterns. If you want to avoid them, explain which ones don't fit your vision.

For example:

"Avoid generic SaaS layouts, purple gradients, excessive rounded cards, floating glass panels, stock-style illustrations, and oversized decorative blobs. Don't make the design look like a startup landing page template."

You don't have to ban every popular design trend from your projects. The point is to identify the visual shortcuts that would make your particular design feel generic.

Negative instructions are especially helpful when you're refining a result that looks technically correct but lacks character.

Just remember that telling an image generator not to include something doesn't guarantee it will obey. AI is a creative assistant, not an employee who has finally read the brief.

### 4. Use visual references

Sometimes describing a design with words is harder than showing what you mean.

If your AI tool supports image references, use them.

You can provide screenshots of websites, photographs, illustrations, magazine layouts, color palettes, or even a collection of unrelated images that capture the mood you're after.

But don't just say, "Make it look like this."

Explain which elements you want to borrow from each reference.

For example:

"Use the typography and editorial composition of the first reference, the warm color palette of the second, and the playful illustration style of the third. Do not copy the layouts directly. Combine these influences into an original design that feels cohesive."

This gives the AI a much clearer job.

You're using references to communicate visual direction, not asking it to reproduce someone else's work pixel by pixel.

### 5. Refine the result instead of starting over

Your first prompt probably won't produce the perfect design.

That's normal.

Think of the first result as a starting point. Once you can see what the AI has created, you'll have a much better idea of what works and what doesn't.

The important part is to give it specific feedback.

Instead of saying:

"Make it better."

Try:

"The layout is too symmetrical and feels like a standard agency website. Keep the color palette and typography, but make the composition more experimental. Move the main heading toward the left, introduce overlapping project previews, and use small handwritten annotations to make the design feel more personal. Reduce the number of decorative elements so the content remains the focus."

Notice that you're not asking the AI to throw everything away.

You're identifying the problem, preserving what works, and explaining what needs to change.

**Good design prompting is an iterative process.** You describe an idea, review the result, identify what's missing, and refine the direction until the design starts to match your vision.

That's also why simply adding more adjectives to a prompt doesn't always help. Specific instructions are more useful than a paragraph full of words like "stunning," "beautiful," "innovative," and "eye-catching."

## A Practical Design Prompt You Can Actually Use

Let's put these ideas together.

Imagine you want to create a visual concept for a personal portfolio website, but you don't want another portfolio that looks like it came straight out of a template generator.

Here's an example prompt you can adapt to your own projects:

**The prompt:**

"Act as an experienced digital art director and web designer. Develop an original visual concept for a personal portfolio website belonging to a freelance creative working across frontend development, animation, and video editing.

**Creative direction:** The website should feel like a combination of an independent design magazine, a personal sketchbook, and a contemporary creative studio. The personality should be playful, thoughtful, and slightly unconventional without sacrificing usability.

**Color palette:** Use a warm off-white background, deep forest-green text, and muted terracotta accents. Introduce additional colors only when they serve a clear visual purpose.

**Typography:** Combine expressive, oversized headlines with a highly readable sans-serif body font. Use occasional handwritten annotations as small decorative details, not as the primary text style.

**Layout:** Create an asymmetrical composition with varied spacing, overlapping visual elements, and project previews that feel individually designed rather than repeated cards. Establish a clear visual hierarchy and leave enough whitespace for the content to breathe.

**Details:** Include subtle paper textures, small hand-drawn marks, and restrained visual imperfections. Use these elements selectively so the design feels intentional rather than messy.

**Avoid:** Generic SaaS layouts, predictable centered hero sections, excessive rounded cards, unnecessary gradients, glassmorphism, and decorative elements that don't contribute to the concept.

**Usability:** Keep navigation intuitive, text readable, and interactive elements clear. Make the layout responsive and ensure the visual concept can realistically be implemented on the web.

Present a cohesive design direction with a homepage layout, typography choices, color usage, and examples of how the visual language carries through to the project section."

This prompt gives the AI enough information to develop a concept while leaving room for creative decisions.

You can make it even more specific by adding screenshots, your own sketches, examples of websites you admire, or details about the people you're designing for.

And if you're using an AI tool that generates actual website code rather than just images, you can ask it to implement the concept using your preferred technologies, such as Astro, React, and SCSS.

Just keep in mind that a beautiful image of a website and a functioning website are two different things. One can look fantastic while the other quietly falls apart when you resize the browser.

## AI Can Generate the Design. You Still Need to Direct It.

There's a bigger point here.

AI makes it easier to generate design ideas, but generating something and making good design decisions aren't the same thing.

You still need to recognize when a layout feels unbalanced, when typography doesn't work, when colors clash, or when a design looks polished but has no connection to the brand it's supposed to represent.

You need to understand why something works, not just whether it looks impressive in a screenshot.

This is where design knowledge becomes particularly valuable.

The more you understand composition, typography, color theory, visual hierarchy, and user experience, the better you can evaluate what AI produces and explain how to improve it.

You don't have to create every element yourself anymore. But you do need to know what you're trying to create.

And that doesn't mean every design needs to be unusual or experimental. Sometimes a simple, familiar layout is exactly what a project needs. The goal isn't to make everything look different just for the sake of it.

The goal is to make intentional decisions instead of accepting the first result because it looks reasonably good.

## Final Thoughts

AI hasn't removed the need for creativity. It's changed how we can explore and execute creative ideas.

You can generate dozens of concepts, experiment with visual directions, and build on ideas that would have taken much longer to explore manually.

But if you keep asking for the same generic things, you'll keep getting familiar-looking results.

The real advantage comes from knowing how to communicate your ideas, recognize what isn't working, and refine the output until it feels right for the project.

So the next time you open an AI design tool, don't just ask it to create something beautiful.

Tell it what the design is for, who it's speaking to, what it should feel like, which visual details matter, and what you want to avoid.

Give it a direction. Give it references. Challenge its first attempt.

**Because AI can generate almost anything. The interesting part is deciding what it's actually worth creating.**
